Inside the experiment

A melon written as a distance

The melon is a signed distance field: an ellipsoid 32 cm long with its belly softly flattened where it lay in the field. Skin, pith and flesh are bands of depth under that surface, so any cut, anywhere, finds the right colour. The stripes are noise wrapped around the long axis, seamless from stem to blossom end.

Pieces

Every piece is the melon clipped by the half-spaces of the cuts that made it, with the fresh edges rounded by a smooth maximum. All pieces keep the melon’s original rest frame, so the rind, the stripes and — seeds stay where they grew. Each piece gets a surface-nets skin, a hexahedral lattice aligned to its own principal axes, and a barycentric embedding that pins one to the other. Seeds are only meshed near cut faces; a seed the blade passes through stays with one side and settles just under the new face.

Physics

XPBD at a fixed 60 Hz with 6 substeps, now — particles in — cells. Each 8-node cell is pulled toward its best-fit rotated rest shape, stiffening as it strains; every tetrahedron keeps its volume. Damping acts on edges and on each piece’s deformation relative to its rigid motion, never on its fall or tumble. Pieces collide against each other’s exact distance field in a best-fit frame, roll to a stop on the table and fall asleep when calm.

Light through jelly

Seeds, bubbles and the knife are drawn first, then the nearest back faces to measure how much jelly each view ray crosses. The front faces refract the scene by that thickness with Beer–Lambert absorption, milky scattering in the pith, a back-lit glow at thin edges, sugar glints, Fresnel studio reflections and GGX highlights. Normals follow the local deformation of the jelly. The key shadow is tinted by whatever the light crossed first.
